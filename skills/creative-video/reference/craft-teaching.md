What teaching videos do that works, why, and the failure each habit prevents.
It is knowledge to apply while you invent your own scenes, not a set of shapes
to pick from: a scene that resembles nothing here and teaches is right. The
`hyperframes` skill's `references/story.md` and `references/types/explainer.md`
cover story shape and the diagram that grows a part at a time; this file adds
the teaching evidence under them. Effect sizes (Cohen's d, Hedges' g) come
from the studies named, mostly short lab lessons with students: read them as
direction and rough size, not a forecast for a 30-minute chaptered film.

## The spectacle carries the idea

Test every vivid thing on screen (a gag, a character, a sound, a camera move)
by naming the part of the concept it maps to. A joke that is the mechanism is
free; a joke beside it is a cost. Removing interesting but irrelevant facts and
clips raised transfer by d = 0.80 (3 of 3 experiments), and removing background
music from a narrated animation by d = 0.95 (2 experiments) (Mayer 2021). A
meta-analysis of seductive details finds g = -0.33 over 58 studies, costing
more when the learner cannot set the pace, which is every video. The best bar
video's strongest moment is the explanation itself: CLAUDE.md as a sticky note
that falls off a fridge while the agent reaches past it, then a padlock that
slams on and bounces it back.

Music is a mood tool for openings, hand-offs and payoffs. Grant Sanderson: in
"the meat of a technical explanation it's very easy for the music to do more
harm than good", so keep it "decidedly in the background". The game show's bed
sat at 0.1 volume under the voice, 0.22 in gaps and 0.34 under the closing
logo; the coherence numbers say to pull it out at the hardest step (levels:
the `hyperframes` skill's `references/sound.md`). This prevents a film that
looks amazing and teaches less than a plain one.

## Stage the wrong idea

Before scripting a chapter, write what a smart newcomer believes about it that
is false or half right. Put that belief on screen as a reasonable view, voiced
by a learner, a rival or the viewer's own guess; treat it on its merits ("why
is this idea so common?"); let it fail visibly; then tie the right idea to the
wrong one it replaces. Sanderson's version: "start with a naive but flawed
solution, and then progressively refine it".

With 364 physics students, a video that stated and refuted the common
misconceptions gained d = 0.79, and a student and tutor dialogue d = 0.83, over
a concise exposition; low prior knowledge learners gained most and high prior
knowledge learners were not harmed (Muller et al. 2008). Students called the
exposition "clear and concise", and it moved novices from 6.0 to 6.3 of 26;
the dialogue nearly doubled them to 11, while viewers rated it more confusing
and reported more effort. Unlike other added material, staged misconceptions
did not interfere. This prevents a film that feels clear and leaves the wrong
belief in place: feeling clear, to the viewer or to you, is not learning, and
the version that teaches may feel harder.

## Ask before you tell

Open a segment on a question the viewer cannot yet answer. Before a reveal,
pose a prediction ("will it run?"), hold a visible beat for a guess, then
answer. At a chapter's end, ask for the idea back, pause, then answer.

Short tests between video segments cut mind wandering to 19% of probes against
39% for a restudy recap and 41% for nothing, and lifted the final test to 90%
against 76% and 68% (d = 1.38); the restudy recap did no better than nothing
(Szpunar et al. 2013). Questions before each segment improved a later test on
the asked and the unasked material alike (Carpenter and Toftness 2017). A
pause for a one-sentence self-explanation gave d = 0.68 (Mayer 2021). Those
learners answered; a video can only pose, and how much survives an unanswered
prompt is unmeasured, so make the beat honest: long enough to guess, and
answerable from what the film has shown. This prevents the replay recap that
teaches nothing, and a viewer who drifts.

## Metaphors and where they break

Find the concept's relations (what grows, what blocks, what flows, what
decides) and look for a familiar thing with the same relations, not the same
look: an analogy maps relations between objects, not their attributes (Gentner
1983). TED-Ed drew Big Data as a tree because both grow, branch and produce.
Map the parts out loud, then say in one line, spoken or on screen, where the
comparison stops being true, above all where the viewer would carry a wrong
inference across. Glynn's model of exemplary teachers' analogies ends on that
step and has the author do every step, because a learner left to do one does
it poorly. Drop a metaphor when it stops paying rather than bend facts to keep
it: Kurzgesagt deleted a popular video that "simplified an idea so much, that
it made a great story, but became distorting".

Draw the subject directly when the thing already shows its working better than
a comparison would: an algorithm running, a proof building, a request moving
through a real pipeline, a matrix moving space. Primer names its invented
parts and why ("I made it a cube because volume scales as the cube of
length"). The game show drew a metaphor as an acted object only where an
action needed showing (the note falls, the lock slams, a trench coat is X-rayed
to reveal `rm -rf`); its other metaphors lived in the host's lines and the card
labels. A metaphor drawn in full where nothing in it acts is decoration. This
prevents a distorted model the viewer keeps, and a forced comparison that costs
more mapping than it saves.

## Build order and worked examples

Open on a concrete question, not a thesis. Give one or two concrete examples
and let the pattern form before naming the abstraction, so the term labels
something already in the viewer's head. Name the parts early and lightly, build
one held picture a part at a time, each part arriving as it is spoken, and
state the general rule last. Sanderson: going abstract first is "like trying
to build a building from the top floor down". Mayer 2021: naming the parts
first d = 0.85, a complex picture built in parts d = 0.67, narration in sync
with its picture d = 1.31, drawing while explaining over pointing at a finished
drawing d = 0.58 (low knowledge learners), signalling by colour or pointing
d = 0.73.

Worked examples help newcomers and get in experts' way (the expertise reversal
effect, Kalyuga et al. 2003). For newcomers, walk one example through every
step, then fade: later examples leave steps for the viewer to anticipate. For
experts, pose the problem and skip the walk. Show the real path, wrong turns
included, and justify each step. Decide the audience first: one repo explained
to its maintainers and to newcomers is two films. This prevents a formula
before its idea, a cold abstraction that loses newcomers, and a walk-through
that bores experts.

## One format concept owns the film

The best bar video chose one concept before any scene, "a game show where you
are the contestant", and every layer obeyed it: the arc (cold open, how to
play, warm-up, rounds, lightning round, final round, scoreboard), the audience
device (five scored questions with a spoken 3-2-1 countdown and an always-on
score display), the cast (a host, and an announcer for round stings), the sound
(a buzzer only for blocked, a ding only for runs, 29 cues on story events), the
colour (green runs, amber asks, red blocked, everywhere) and the furniture
(spotlights, marquee bulbs, round banners). The agent wrote its own 499-line
show kit and 12 scenes of about 104 lines each for that one film.

Why it works (inferred): a concept gives each colour and each sound one meaning
the viewer learns once and then reads without the narration, and its device
can teach in itself (answering before the countdown ends is a prediction). The
other bar, a 34-minute explainer, filled 14 fixed component types from YAML;
its strength was structure (a progress rail, chapter cards, a citation behind
every sentence), and its motion sat in the same low band as a slide deck. This
prevents the film with no concept, which falls back to hook, points, recap.

## Characters with a job

A character earns its place by acting, gesturing or holding a view. Three jobs
have evidence: the learner who holds the wrong idea (Muller's dialogue,
d = 0.83); the agent that follows the system's rules so the viewer watches the
mechanism play out (Primer's blobs); the guide who gestures at what is being
explained (an on-screen agent that gestures while talking d = 0.76 over 8
experiments, a static instructor image beside the content d = -0.25). Give the
explanation a story shape: someone wants something, meets an obstacle, and each
fix causes the next problem; Willingham's four Cs are causality, conflict,
complications and character, conflict mattering most. A second voice is cheap:
the game show's announcer was a second instruct string on the same local TTS,
spoke five lines, all format stings, with a 0.7 s gap at a change of speaker
against 0.38 s within one. This prevents a mascot standing beside the content,
which measured worse than no character.

## Chapters and length

Engagement falls sharply past about 6 minutes per video (Guo et al. 2014, 6.9
million edX sessions; engagement is watch time, not learning). A long film is
a chain of short chapters, each with its own question and payoff; the
34-minute bar explainer ran nine chapters of about 3.75 minutes each. This
prevents a 30-minute monologue the viewer leaves at minute seven.

## Pictures on the word

Change the picture on the word that names the change, several times in one
sentence when it names several things, and build inside a held frame rather
than cut. The game show changed the picture 15.2 times a minute with 18% of
frames in motion; the 34-minute explainer 1.6 and 7%; three `explainer-video`
renders 0.9 to 3.9 and 5 to 7%; a slide-deck video by the same agent 4.6 and
8%. Hard cuts stayed between 0.0 and 0.6 a minute in all of them (measured with
ffmpeg scene scores at 10 fps: an event is a rise to 0.02, a moving step one
scoring 0.002). The game show placed each event at a narration line's start
plus a hand-tuned offset: its fridge scene ran about ten events in 32 s, and
the countdown ticks landed on the spoken "three, two, one". This skill anchors
events to aligned word times instead (`craft-motion.md`). Narration in sync
with its picture is Mayer's largest effect here, d = 1.31.

The 15 a minute is what one strong film did, not a target: its type was large
(headlines 76 to 84 px) and every event was a story event. This prevents a
picture that lags the voice, changes once a sentence, or holds still while
three things are named.

## Voice and pace

Write narration as talk to one viewer ("your request", not "the request"):
conversational wording gave d = 1.00 (5 of 5 experiments). Both bar videos ran
at 153 to 154 words a minute. Engagement rose with speaking rate up to 254
words a minute, read as enthusiasm rather than speed (Guo et al. 2014), while
Sanderson speaks "slowly and deliberately, giving viewers plenty of
opportunities to pause and ponder". They resolve by content: brisk through the
familiar, slow at the new step, with a visible pause there. A modern TTS
engine matched a recorded human voice on learning and credibility (Craig and
Schroeder 2017, 2018), so a good synthetic narrator costs nothing.

Spoken words beside a picture beat printed ones (d = 1.02) for first-language
listeners, and the reverse holds for second-language listeners; captions that
repeat the narration cost native listeners (d = 0.80 against), so keep
captions out of the diagram's space. Put a label on the part it names (d = 0.65
over a caption), and never print the spoken sentence word for word. This
prevents a lecture read aloud, and an even, slow delivery with no hard step
marked.

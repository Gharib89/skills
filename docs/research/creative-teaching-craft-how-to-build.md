# Teaching craft: how to build it

Part of [Teaching craft of the best animated explainers](creative-teaching-craft.md),
research for [#969](https://github.com/Gharib89/skills/issues/969). Sources
named in full in that file or its sibling are cited here by short name.

Labels: **documented** (a primary source says so, linked: the creator's own
words or the researchers' own paper), **reported** (a secondary source quotes or
summarises the primary one), **inferred** (my reading for this skill, no direct
proof).

## 5. Build order

**Craft.** Open on a concrete question or puzzle, not a thesis. Give one or two
concrete examples and let the pattern form before naming the abstraction, so
the formal term arrives to label something already in the viewer's head.
Introduce the names of the parts early and lightly. Then build one held picture
a part at a time, each part arriving as its sentence is spoken.

**Evidence.**

- Grant Sanderson: "try very hard to structure your explanation to go from the
  concrete to the abstract"; the natural first-draft habit is the reverse,
  which is "like trying to build a building from the top floor down"
  (documented: SoME1 announcement, linked in [what to show](creative-teaching-craft-what-to-show.md)). On microscale motivation: before a
  formula appears, its idea should already "exist at least loosely in the
  viewer's mind"; motivate with "clear examples, not sweeping statements"; and
  he regrets having "over-philosophized" introductions (documented: SoME2
  results, linked in [what to show](creative-teaching-craft-what-to-show.md)). Clear entries "keep one or two examples front and center",
  playing with edge cases before general rules (same source).
- Vox (Joss Fong): open fast, and withhold the result until the viewer
  understands why it is hard (reported in #656 from [The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2020/01/07/videogram-how-a-vox-video-explains-the-science-behind-the-first-photo-of-a-black-hole/)).
- Mayer 2021 (documented): pre-training in the names and behaviour of the
  parts before a fast lesson, d = 0.85 (2 experiments); segmenting, a complex
  slide built up in parts, d = 0.67 (7 of 7); temporal contiguity, narration in
  sync with the matching picture, d = 1.31 (8 of 8); an instructor drawing
  while explaining beat pointing at a finished drawing, d = 0.58 for
  low-knowledge learners; signalling by colour or pointing, d = 0.73
  (10 experiments).
- Agrees with #773's measurement that narrated explainers build a frame
  rather than cut (documented there).

**For the skill (inferred).** These are the strongest effects in the file and
they already match the plumbing (narration-locked cues). The craft the agent
adds is order: question, example, name, build, then the general rule.

## 6. Pacing and length

**Craft.** Keep each unit short and dense, carried by enthusiasm rather than
slowed delivery, and slow down only at the step that needs thought, with a
visible pause to "pause and ponder". Long material is a chain of short
chapters, each with its own question and payoff.

**Evidence.**

- edX study of 6.9 million video sessions: engagement drops sharply past about
  6 minutes, so "invest in pre-production to plan for under 6 minutes";
  engagement rose with speaking rate up to 254 wpm, which the authors read as
  enthusiasm rather than speed; slow speakers who drew while talking also held
  attention; tablet-drawn tutorials held viewers 1.5 to 2 times longer than
  slides (documented: [Guo, Kim, Rubin 2014](https://cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)).
  Engagement is watch time, not learning.
- Grant Sanderson speaks "slowly and deliberately, giving viewers plenty of
  opportunities to pause and ponder" and tests scripts on a friend or two in a
  sample lesson to calibrate detail (reported: [AMS Notices 2022](https://www.ams.org/journals/notices/202210/noti2559/noti2559.html)).
- Mayer's segmenting (d = 0.67) was learner-paced; a video is system-paced,
  which is where seductive details cost most ([what to show](creative-teaching-craft-what-to-show.md), item 1).

**For the skill (inferred).** The ship explainer's nine chapters over 33:49
(about 3.75 minutes each) already sit inside the 6-minute band. The tension
between Guo's energy and Grant's deliberate pace resolves by content: brisk
through the familiar, slow at the new step.

## 7. Characters and story

**Craft.** Give the explanation a story shape: someone (a person, a packet, a
blob) wants something, meets an obstacle, and each fix causes the next problem.
Let characters show what they are through action, and give them a job in the
explanation (holding a wrong idea, following the system's rules, asking the
viewer's question). A character that only stands there is worse than none.

**Evidence.**

- Willingham: stories are easier to understand and remember; their "four Cs"
  are causality, conflict, complications and character; conflict matters most,
  and screenwriters spend the first fifth of a film building interest before
  the main conflict (documented: [Willingham, "The Privileged Status of
  Story", American Educator 2004](https://www.aft.org/ae/summer2004/willingham)).
- Mayer 2021 (documented): an on-screen agent that gestures while talking,
  d = 0.76 (8 experiments); eye contact, d = 0.60 (4); a static image of the
  instructor next to the slides, d = -0.25 (2).
- Kurzgesagt: "quirky and relatable characters" are a trademark, such as
  sympathetic mitochondria and brown dwarfs (documented: kurzgesagt.org/youtube).
- Primer's blobs are agents that follow stated rules, so the viewer watches
  the mechanism play out ("let's just watch these creatures live their lives")
  (documented: Simulating Natural Selection, linked in [what to show](creative-teaching-craft-what-to-show.md)).
- Muller's Dialogue ([what to show](creative-teaching-craft-what-to-show.md), item 2) is a two-character scene with a measured
  learning gain.

**For the skill (inferred).** Three character jobs have support: the learner
who is wrong (Muller), the rule-following agent (Primer), and the gesturing
guide (Mayer's embodiment). A host and announcer, as in the game show, fit the
guide job if they move and point at the thing being explained.

## 8. Voice and wording

**Craft.** Write narration as talk to one person ("your request", not "the
request"). Keep words spoken rather than printed over the picture, and put any
on-screen label next to the thing it names.

**Evidence.**

- Mayer 2021 (documented): conversational wording, d = 1.00 (5 of 5); spoken
  rather than printed words with graphics, d = 1.02 (7 of 7) for first-language
  learners, reversed for second-language learners, who learned better with
  printed words; captions repeating the narration, d = 0.80 against them
  (4 experiments); labels placed on the graphic rather than in a caption,
  d = 0.65; a pleasant human voice over a machine voice, d = 0.74 (6 of 7).
- The voice result came from old engines: a modern TTS engine produced more
  transfer learning than an old one and matched a recorded human voice on
  learning and credibility (documented: [Craig and Schroeder 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.07.003),
  [2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0735633118802877)).
- Mayer 2017's summary values for the same principles differ slightly
  (coherence 0.70, signalling 0.46, redundancy 0.87, spatial contiguity 0.79,
  temporal contiguity 1.30, segmenting 0.70, pre-training 0.46, modality 0.72,
  personalisation 0.79, voice 0.74, embodiment 0.36, multimedia 1.67) because
  they pool a different set of e-learning studies (documented: [Mayer 2017,
  JCAL 33:403-423](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12197) abstract).

**For the skill (inferred).** Captions stay on for access, which the redundancy
result cuts against for native listeners and supports for second-language
listeners; keeping captions out of the diagram's space limits the cost. The
own-voice and Egyptian Arabic plumbing gets no penalty from the voice principle
as long as the engine is modern.

## 9. Worked examples and the audience's level

**Craft.** For a newcomer, walk one example through every step before asking
for any generalisation, then fade: later examples leave steps for the viewer to
anticipate. For an expert audience, skip the full walk-through and pose the
problem. Decide the audience first, because the same scene helps one and slows
the other.

**Evidence.**

- Worked examples beat problem solving for novices, then the advantage
  disappears and reverses as expertise grows; fading worked steps out
  gradually beats an abrupt switch (documented: [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler,
  Sweller 2003, "The Expertise Reversal Effect"](http://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2003-Kalyuga_et_al.pdf),
  citing Renkl and Atkinson).
- Mayer: several principles are "stronger for low- rather than high-knowledge
  learners" (documented: Mayer 2017 abstract); the drawing effect was measured
  on low-knowledge students (Mayer 2021).
- Grant: a worked problem works best when it shows the real path, wrong turns
  included, and justifies each step (documented: SoME2 results, linked in [what to show](creative-teaching-craft-what-to-show.md)).

**For the skill (inferred).** The skill's input could carry an audience level
the agent uses to pick between a full walk-through and a posed problem; the
same repo explained to its maintainers and to newcomers is two different
videos.

# Teaching craft: what to show

Part of [Teaching craft of the best animated explainers](creative-teaching-craft.md),
research for [#969](https://github.com/Gharib89/skills/issues/969). Sources
named in full in that file or its sibling are cited here by short name.

Labels: **documented** (a primary source says so, linked: the creator's own
words or the researchers' own paper), **reported** (a secondary source quotes or
summarises the primary one), **inferred** (my reading for this skill, no direct
proof).

## 1. Spectacle that carries the idea

**Craft.** Every vivid thing on screen (a gag, a character, a sound, a camera
move) either *is* part of the explanation or competes with it. Test each one by
asking which part of the concept it maps to. A metaphor scene where the joke is
the mechanism (a padlock that a sticky note cannot hold against) is free; a
joke beside the mechanism is a cost.

**Evidence.**

- Mayer's review of instructional video: adding interesting but irrelevant
  facts and clips to a lightning lesson lowered transfer; excluding them gave
  a median d = 0.80 across 3 of 3 experiments, and removing background music
  from a narrated animation gave d = 0.95 across two experiments (documented:
  [Mayer 2021, JARMAC 10:229-240, author manuscript](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/572724/mod_resource/content/2/1-s2.0-S2211368121000231-am.pdf),
  read with `pdftotext`).
- Seductive details meta-analysis: 54 studies, 72 effect sizes, 5,111
  participants, g = -0.43 overall, about -0.28 after correcting for
  publication bias (documented: [Sundararajan 2018 dissertation](http://hdl.handle.net/2376/16327)).
  The published version reports g = -0.33 over 58 studies and a larger cost
  when learners cannot control the pace, which is every video (reported:
  [INSPIRE summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details) of
  [Sundararajan and Adesope 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4);
  the paper itself is paywalled). Rey's 2012 meta-analysis: retention d = -0.30,
  transfer d = -0.48 (reported in the dissertation above).
- Grant Sanderson: music "can enrich the storytelling aspect", but "once you're
  getting into the meat of a technical explanation it's very easy for the music
  to do more harm than good"; if present, "decidedly in the background"
  (documented: [SoME2 results video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cDofhN-RJqg),
  [transcript](https://pickscribe.com/v/cDofhN-RJqg)).
- Kurzgesagt takes the other side for mood: each video gets an original score
  composed to the finished cut "to highlight the important parts", and sound
  effects "add an additional layer" (documented: [How to Make a Kurzgesagt
  Video in 1200 Hours](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uFk0mgljtns),
  [transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/kurzgesagt/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-1200-hours)).
- Grant on where value comes from: "content is king"; the choice of topic and
  how it is explained "determines the majority of the value", yet creators
  spend "about one percent of your time" choosing it (documented: [SoME1
  announcement](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ojjzXyQCzso)).

**For the skill (inferred).** The ambition stance can ask for amazement *in the
explanation*: a better angle, a metaphor that is the mechanism, a surprise the
concept itself produces. Music and effects are mood tools for openings,
transitions and payoffs; under a dense technical beat they are a cost the
agent should weigh, which feeds the map's "cast and sound design" question.
The bar videos already fit this: their metaphors are load-bearing.

## 2. Stage the wrong idea

**Craft.** Before scripting, list what this audience probably believes that is
wrong or half right. Put that belief on screen as a real, reasonable view (said
by a learner character, or stated and tested), show where it fails, and let
the right idea replace it. A version that only states the right idea can feel
clear and still leave the wrong one in place.

**Evidence.**

- 364 first-year physics students, four randomly assigned videos on Newton's
  laws: the Refutation (misconceptions stated and refuted) and the Dialogue (a
  student and tutor talk through them) gained d = 0.79 and d = 0.83 over the
  concise Exposition; low prior knowledge learners gained most and high prior
  knowledge learners were not harmed (documented: [Muller, Bewes, Sharma,
  Reimann 2008, JCAL 24:144-155](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x)).
  The authors conclude that misconceptions, unlike other added material, do
  not interfere with learning.
- Muller, in his own words: the Exposition followed Mayer's principles, students
  called it "clear and concise" and "easy to understand", and novices went from
  6.0 to 6.3 of 26; the Dialogue nearly doubled them to 11, and viewers rated
  it more confusing and reported more mental effort (6 against 5 of 9)
  (documented: [Muller's guest post](https://fnoschese.wordpress.com/2011/03/15/what-puts-the-pseudo-in-pseudoteaching/)).
  His advice there: present the misconception first, treat it on its merits
  ("why is this idea so common?"), and tie the scientific idea to it.
- Grant Sanderson praises the same move as "start with a naive but flawed
  solution, and then progressively refine it", and a puzzle video that shows
  "the wrong turns and tangents" (documented: SoME2 results, above).

**For the skill (inferred).** This answers part of the map's cast question: a
learner voice that holds the wrong idea is a teaching device with evidence, not
only flavour. It also warns that the owner's "this felt clear and amazing" is
not proof of learning; the storyboard could show which misconception each
chapter confronts.

## 3. Retrieval and prediction instead of replay

**Craft.** Ask before you tell, and ask again after. Open a segment with a
question the viewer cannot yet answer; before a reveal, ask them to predict;
at a chapter's end, ask for the idea back, leave a beat, then answer. A recap
that simply replays the content is the weakest form.

**Evidence.**

- Video lecture with short tests between segments: mind wandering 19% of
  probes against 39% (restudy) and 41% (none); final cumulative test 90%
  against 76% and 68%, d = 1.38; the restudy group did no better than the
  untested group (documented: [Szpunar, Khan, Schacter 2013, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1221764110)).
- Prequestions before each of three video segments improved a later test on
  both the asked and the unasked material (documented: [Carpenter and Toftness
  2017, JARMAC](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2016.07.014)).
- Mayer's generative activity principle: pausing after each slide for a
  one-sentence self-explanation, d = 0.68 in 3 of 3 experiments (documented:
  Mayer 2021).
- Practice: Primer asks "what would you predict?" before unpausing a
  simulation, then reports honestly when the result surprised the author, and
  ends with "a quick recap" (documented: [Simulating Natural Selection](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0ZGbIKd0XrM),
  [transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/primerblobs/simulating-natural-selection)).
  TED-Ed pairs every animation with a "Think" section of questions (documented:
  [TED-Ed lesson pages](https://ed.ted.com/lessons/making-a-ted-ed-lesson-visualizing-complex-ideas)).
  Grant judges entries on whether the viewer knows "within the first 30
  seconds why they should care" (documented: [SoME1 rules](https://www.3blue1brown.com/blog/some1/)).

**For the skill (inferred).** A linear video cannot collect an answer, so the
evidence transfers only partly: the studies had learners respond. The nearest
honest form is a posed question with a visible pause before the answer (a
"will it run?" frame is exactly this). This feeds the map's "audience devices"
question with evidence for prediction and retrieval frames and against a
replay recap.

## 4. Choosing a metaphor and keeping it honest

**Craft.** Find the concept's *relations* (what grows, what blocks, what flows,
what decides) and look for a familiar thing with the same relations, not the
same look. Then map the parts out loud, and say where the borrowing stops,
especially where the viewer would otherwise carry a wrong inference across.
Drop the metaphor when it stops paying, rather than bending facts to keep it.

**Evidence.**

- TED-Ed's animators on Big Data: the script's servers "branch out into bigger
  networks to produce data", which led to a tree whose leaves pile up faster
  than they can be collected; style then followed the story's era (documented:
  [Making a TED-Ed Lesson: Visualizing big ideas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P50NcJLSJJY)).
- Glynn's teaching-with-analogies model, from task analysis of exemplary
  teachers: introduce target, cue the analog, identify relevant features, map
  similarities, indicate where the analogy breaks down, draw conclusions; the
  author should do all six, because a learner left to do one may do it poorly
  (documented: [Glynn, Teaching Science with Analogies, NRRC report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED378554.pdf)).
- Gentner's structure-mapping theory: an analogy maps relations between
  objects, not the objects' attributes (documented: Gentner 1983, *Cognitive
  Science* 7(2):155-170, [doi:10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3);
  cited from the paper's abstract, not re-read for this ticket).
- Kurzgesagt: "the hardest part is being brief, while not simplifying too
  much"; scripts are about 1,300 words, so "there's always some degree of
  simplification"; experts fact-check the script twice, and "many scripts die"
  there; they deleted two popular videos, one because "we simplified an idea so
  much, that it made a great story, but became distorting" (documented: [Can
  You Trust Kurzgesagt Videos?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JtUAAXe_0VI)).
  Their process page: visual metaphors are chosen in a sketching phase after
  the script is final (documented: [kurzgesagt.org/youtube](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)).
- Primer names its model's invented parts and why: "I could have picked any
  function for the energy cost I suppose but I made it a cube because volume
  scales as the cube of length" (documented: Simulating Natural Selection,
  above).

**For the skill (inferred).** This is the craft half of the map's "facts traced
to sources while metaphors stay free" question: the metaphor is free, its
mapping is checked against the source, and its breaking point is said. A
storyboard card could carry a one-line "maps X to Y, breaks at Z".
